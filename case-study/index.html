Hearts for Low Vision

My mom plays Hearts on her iPhone. She has a visual impairment, and her commercial Hearts app fans all 13 cards into one overlapping row under a giant instruction box. I directed Claude Code to design and build a version made for her eyes, and I made every visual decision from side-by-side options at true phone size.

The user and the problem

Thirteen cards on a small screen

Hearts deals each player 13 cards. Commercial apps overlap them in a single fan, so each card shows only a thin strip, and her rank and suit have to be read from that sliver. On a phone 390 points wide, the design has to show every card whole, stay readable when one suit runs long, and keep every tap target large.

13

Cards in a hand, every one fully visible and tappable.

390 pt

Screen width of an iPhone 16e, held upright.

4 rows

One per suit, so a suit is always in the same place.

44 pt+

Smallest tap target, for slow, deliberate taps.

7.2:1

Red rank contrast on a card, above WCAG AAA.

How I ran it

Rules I set

  • Every visual decision is a webpage. "Show me in a webpage anytime I need to make a visual decision," opened for me automatically, with the current version always among the options.
  • Measure, don't estimate. Contrast ratios are measured from the rendered screen, and every layout is checked at true phone size.
  • Each AI keeps its own site. My shared games site is "reserved for the winner of each new game," copied in only after I choose.
  • Public pages keep her anonymous. Describe her condition "in vague terms to preserve patient anonymity."
  • Her fixes carry forward. What her playtest taught about tapping is "applied to every game you create," from the first build.

Design rounds

From first design to shipped game

1

One row per suit

Claude's design, one entry in a three-way design comparison, puts her hand in four rows: clubs, diamonds, spades, hearts. When she must follow suit, the cards she can play are one whole row. Each player's card lands under their name, labeled "Led", "Winning" or "Takes it". Claude made its entry after seeing the other two and labeled it that way. I chose it.

OutcomeSelected Suit Rows.
2

A real hand tested the design

During the review, my mom was dealt seven cards of one suit in a game she was playing, so I asked to see how the design would handle it. It failed: seven cards in one row overlap, and the next card clipped the 10 and covered half of each suit. The fix sizes every rank and suit to fit inside the strip that stays visible. Seven of one suit comes up in roughly 1 hand in 25.

Rule lockedEvery card keeps its rank and suit inside the visible strip.
3

From prototype to full game

Claude built the complete game: passing left, right and across, hearts breaking, shooting the moon, three computer players, a save after every move, offline play, and English, Spanish and Vietnamese. The rules were tested with 5,000 simulated games (54,865 hands), and the screen with automated tap tests at four iPhone sizes, run again against the live site after every release.

OutcomeFull game live.
4

Delight, without moving anything

I asked whether the game was Claude's best effort, with delight throughout the gameplay while staying low-vision friendly. Claude said no: it was correct and readable but plain. The layout stayed fixed. Claude added motion that explains (each card glides out of its player's name, and a finished trick sweeps to the winner), a gentle sound for each moment, announcements when hearts are broken or the queen of spades is played, celebrations sized to the moment, and a memory of games won and best score. I chose sky lanterns for a win and heart cards for a clean hand, and asked for a new design for shooting the moon.

OutcomeMotion, sound and celebrations, each skipped or stilled when the phone asks for reduced motion.
5

Checked against WCAG

To show which cards she can play, I first chose a gold outline plus a light dim on the rest. Then I asked Claude to "check the WCAG contrast guidelines against this option and give me a few other options to choose from with WCAG in mind." Measured from the screen, red ranks on dimmed cards were 4.3:1: AA, just short of AAA. I chose a deeper red ink, which reaches AAA on every card (7.2:1, and 5.4:1 dimmed).

Rule lockedRed ranks meet WCAG AAA on every card, dimmed or not.
6

The Home Screen icon

Six icon options were shown at real Home Screen size next to her Solitaire icon, on dark and light wallpapers. I chose the ace and queen, asked for six variations with switches for the selection glow and a border, and chose the first with both turned on.

OutcomeIcon shipped.
User observation and adjustment

Watching her play

Her first playtest showed how she really touches the screen and reads the cards. Each thing I saw became a change in the game, and the first two became rules for every game Claude builds for her.

What I observed

She holds her finger on the screen longer than most people. Instead of choosing the card, the long press selected text on the screen.

Adjustment

A long hold counts as a tap, and nothing in the game can be selected as text, dialogs included.

How it was checked

Automated tests: a long hold counts once, and a quick tap right after one is not lost.

What I observed

Her finger drifts. Pressing "Play the 5 of hearts," she dragged up a little before lifting, and the tap didn't register.

Adjustment

A press that starts on a card or button counts if it lifts on it or within about a finger's width (44 px).

How it was checked

Automated tests: a small upward slide off the Play button counts once, and a long slide away does not.

What I observed

Hearts were led, and she struggled to tell her hearts (playable) from her diamonds (not playable): red next to red.

Adjustment

A card she can't play shakes "no" when tapped, the cards she can play hop twice, and the phone buzzes twice, even on silent.

How it was checked

Automated tests of the shake and hop; the buzz has to be felt on her phone.

What I observed

After a pass, the three new cards vanished from the table with nothing to show where they went.

Adjustment

The three cards now glide from the table into their places in her hand.

How it was checked

Automated tests: each place stays empty until its card lands, even if the screen resizes mid-flight.

Rule lockedFor every game: "accessibility changes that need to be applied to every game you create."
